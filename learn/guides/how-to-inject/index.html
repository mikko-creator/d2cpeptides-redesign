How subcutaneous injections are given

A subcutaneous injection places medicine in the fatty tissue just under the skin, not in muscle.1 This guide describes how prescribed subcutaneous injections are given, following patient guides from the US National Library of Medicine, the American Diabetes Association, the NHS and FDA: sites, rotation, clean technique and sharps disposal. It is not a guide to injecting any research compound.

What a subcutaneous injection is

A subcutaneous (SQ or sub-Q) injection goes into the fatty layer under the skin. MedlinePlus, the National Library of Medicine's patient site, lists insulin, blood thinners and fertility drugs among the medicines people give themselves this way.1 The NHS describes semaglutide and tirzepatide as once-weekly injections that patients give themselves on prescription.23

Injection is the usual route for peptide medicines, for a practical reason: a 2022 review explains that digestive enzymes in the stomach and intestine break peptides down, so most peptide drugs are given by injection.10

Research peptides are a different case. Under federal rules, an unapproved new drug sent out for in vitro or animal research has to be labeled with a caution that ends "Not for use in humans".11 Everything below describes prescribed medicines. For any of them, the instructions supplied with the product and the prescriber's directions come first.

Where the injections go

MedlinePlus names three areas for self-injection:1

  • Belly: between the bottom of the ribs and the hip bones, keeping 2 inches (5.1 cm) or more from the belly button.
  • Thighs: the outer side of the upper thigh.
  • Upper arms: the back or outer side, 3 inches (7.6 cm) or more below the shoulder and above the elbow.

The American Diabetes Association names the same three areas, abdomen, thigh and arm, for insulin given by syringe, pen or pump.12 NHS medicine pages for weekly semaglutide and tirzepatide name the thigh, tummy and upper arm, and for tirzepatide the back of the upper arm, where someone else may need to help. People who also inject insulin are told to use a different place for these medicines.23

The skin itself matters. Only healthy skin is used, with nothing red, swollen, scarred or bruised.1 The MedlinePlus insulin guide also keeps shots 1 inch (2.5 cm) clear of scars and rules out tender, lumpy, firm or numb spots.4

Pen medicines come with their own Instructions for Use, covered on the Ozempic, Wegovy and Mounjaro pen pages.

Rotating sites

MedlinePlus spaces each injection 1 inch or more from the one before, to protect the skin and aid absorption.1 For weekly semaglutide or tirzepatide, the NHS says the same area can be used each week, but not the exact same spot.23 The MedlinePlus insulin guide suggests keeping a chart of the spots already used.4 It names injections into lumpy, firm or numb tissue as a frequent reason insulin acts less well than it should.4

The steps patient guides teach

The MedlinePlus guides break a syringe injection into the steps below. Prefilled pens skip the vial steps and follow their own instructions.

  1. Wash your hands with soap under running water for at least 1 minute, then dry them on a clean paper towel.1
  2. Check the medicine: the right medicine, the right dose, and the date, so it is not out of date.15
  3. Inspect the vial for a change in color, floating particles or cloudiness. Wipe its rubber top with an alcohol pad and let it dry without blowing on it.54 A vial used more than once is dated when it is first opened.5
  4. Draw up the dose. Pull air into the syringe to the dose line and push it into the vial, which stops a vacuum building up inside. Turn the vial upside down with the needle tip in the liquid and pull the plunger back to the dose line. Tap any bubbles to the top, push them back into the vial, and check the amount again.5
  5. Prepare the skin. Here the guides differ. The subcutaneous guide cleans the site with an alcohol pad, wiping in a circle outward, and lets it dry.1 The insulin guide asks for clean, dry skin, washes visibly dirty skin with soap and water, and leaves the alcohol wipe off the site.4 The product's own instructions settle it.
  6. Pinch up roughly an inch (2.5 cm) of skin and the fat beneath it, without the muscle.1
  7. Insert the needle quickly. The angle differs too: 90 degrees in the subcutaneous guide, or 45 degrees where there is little fat,1 and 45 degrees by default in the insulin guide, with 90 degrees only for thicker tissue and with the provider's agreement.4
  8. Inject and withdraw. Release the skin and push the plunger all the way down.1 The insulin guide leaves the syringe in place for 5 seconds, then withdraws the needle at its angle of entry.4
  9. Press clean gauze on the spot for a few seconds.1
  10. Discard the needle and syringe straight into a sharps container.8

One needle, one syringe, one time

MedlinePlus says never to reuse needles or syringes.14 The American Diabetes Association notes that makers do not vouch for a syringe's sterility once it has been reused, and says never to let anyone use a syringe you have used, or to use anyone else's. It adds that wiping a needle with alcohol strips the coating that helps it slide into the skin.13 A syringe with a torn wrapper, or a cap that is broken or missing, goes into a sharps container unused.1

How to read unit marks, and why syringe units are not milligrams, is covered in dosing basics. For lab measurements, the reconstitution calculator and the peptide calculator show the arithmetic. Syringes, needles and alcohol swabs are listed on the supplies page.

After an injection

MedlinePlus says some soreness at the site is expected, and advises contacting a health care provider for signs of infection, such as redness, swelling, red streaks or fever.1

Sharps disposal

FDA's term "sharps" covers any device with a point or edge that can pierce or cut skin: needles, syringes, lancets, auto-injectors such as insulin and epinephrine pens, infusion sets and connection needles. Used needles that are not disposed of safely can injure people and spread infections, most commonly hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV.6

FDA's method has two steps: sharps go into a disposal container immediately after use, and the full container is then disposed of under your community's rules.8

Choosing a container

  • An FDA-cleared container is rigid plastic with a fill line, available from pharmacies, medical supply companies and health care providers, or online.67
  • Lacking one, FDA says a heavy-duty plastic household container can serve, for example a laundry detergent bottle that resists leaks, stands upright and closes with a tight, puncture-resistant lid. Every container should be labeled to warn of hazardous waste inside.67
  • The ADA also accepts an opaque, heavy-duty plastic bottle that screws shut, or a firmly closing box of plastic or metal, and says the container should not go in the recycling.13
  • FDA suggests asking the drug maker whether it offers a free sharps container, and asking a medical or prescription insurer whether containers are covered.9

When the container is three-quarters full

Once a container is about three-quarters full, FDA says to dispose of it under community rules, since overfilling invites needle-sticks, and never to reuse it. Options depend on where you live: drop boxes or supervised collection sites at doctors' offices, hospitals, pharmacies, health departments, or police and fire stations; household hazardous waste sites; mail-back programs for certain FDA-cleared containers; and residential special waste pick-up. For state-specific rules, FDA points to the Safe Needle Disposal program.8

What never to do

  • Never drop loose needles into trash or recycling bins, at home or in public, and never flush them. Needles cannot be recycled.69
  • Never remove, bend, break or recap a needle used by another person, and never remove a needle without a needle clipper.9
  • If no container is at hand, recap only with a mechanical device or the one-handed method. Needle clippers work for small syringes like insulin syringes, but not for lancets.14
  • Store sharps and their containers where children and pets cannot reach them.89

Needle-stick first aid

FDA's advice after a stick from someone else's used needle: wash the spot at once with soap and water, or use an antiseptic such as rubbing alcohol or hand sanitizer, then get medical care straight away from a physician or a local hospital. Blood or other body fluids in the eyes, nose or mouth, or on the skin, call for the same steps.6

Traveling with syringes

FDA advises carrying a travel-size sharps container, checking the TSA website before flying, keeping each medicine labeled with what it is and who made it, or with a pharmacy label, and bringing a letter from a doctor.8 The ADA says to bring used syringes home in a hard case, such as a plastic pencil box.13

Frequently asked questions

Where do you give a subcutaneous injection?

MedlinePlus names three areas: the belly, at least 2 inches from the belly button; the outer upper thighs; and the side or back of the upper arms, at least 3 inches below the shoulder and above the elbow.1 NHS medicine pages for weekly semaglutide and tirzepatide name the thigh, tummy and upper arm.23 The skin should be healthy, and injections are spaced at least an inch apart.1 The instructions supplied with a prescribed product take priority.

Should you use an alcohol swab before a subcutaneous injection?

The patient guides differ. The MedlinePlus subcutaneous guide cleans the skin with an alcohol pad and lets it dry,1 while its insulin guide asks only for clean, dry skin and rules out an alcohol wipe on the injection site.4 Both agree on wiping a vial's rubber top with alcohol before a needle goes through it.45 Where they disagree, the instructions that come with the prescribed medicine decide.

What angle is a subcutaneous injection given at?

Guides give two defaults. The MedlinePlus subcutaneous guide inserts the needle quickly at 90 degrees, or at 45 degrees when there is little fatty tissue.1 The MedlinePlus insulin guide starts at 45 degrees and goes straight in at 90 degrees only when the tissue is thicker and the provider agrees.4 Both pinch up the skin first; the subcutaneous guide takes about an inch (2.5 cm) of skin and fat, not muscle.14

How do you get rid of used needles at home?

Put them straight into a sharps container: an FDA-cleared one, or a heavy-duty plastic bottle, such as a laundry detergent jug, with a tight, puncture-resistant lid and a hazardous-waste label.67 At about three-quarters full, local rules apply; options can include drop-off points, household hazardous waste sites, mail-back programs and pick-up services.8 Never put loose needles in the trash or recycling, and never flush them.9
