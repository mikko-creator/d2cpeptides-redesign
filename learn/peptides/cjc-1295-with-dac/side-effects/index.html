Side effects

CJC-1295 with DAC side effects

In small studies of healthy adults, the most frequent side effects of CJC-1295 with DAC were injection-site reactions, headache, diarrhea and flushing.1 After one dose, 94% of people on the drug reported at least one adverse event, against 29% on placebo.2 A participant in a halted patient trial died of a heart attack.3

These figures come from two placebo-controlled trials in healthy adults, one with 42 people given a single dose and one with 24 people given repeated doses, and from a study of 12 healthy men.21819 The list runs from most to least common.17

Injection-site reactions tended to be more severe or to last longer at higher doses.1 In the repeated-dose trial, flushing occurred only on the drug, started within 30 minutes of an injection and cleared within 1 to 2 hours.1 The doses behind these figures are on the CJC-1295 with DAC dosage page.

Serious events

No serious adverse reactions were reported in the healthy-volunteer studies, and blood and urine tests, including glucose and liver tests, and ECGs showed no consistent changes.111 FDA nonetheless lists increased heart rate and systemic vasodilatory reactions among serious adverse events associated with CJC-1295.8 The one patient trial, in adults with HIV-associated abdominal fat, was terminated after a participant died of a heart attack, and FDA found no further information about the other participants or their adverse events.3

Real-world reports are scarce. FDA's search of its adverse event database (FAERS) through June 10, 2024 found only two CJC-1295 reports: one was excluded for insufficient information and the other reported no adverse event. A literature search through June 11, 2024 found no relevant US case reports.2

Warnings from FDA's review

  • Growth hormone and IGF-1 effects: approved growth hormone labels list an increased risk of tumors, glucose intolerance and diabetes, raised pressure inside the skull, fluid retention, low adrenal and thyroid function, and pancreatitis. FDA has no data suggesting CJC-1295 would avoid these risks.7
  • Immune reactions: injected peptides can trigger antibodies, and CJC-1295 shares 86% of its sequence with the body's own GHRH, so antibodies could cross-react with it. The original study found no significant antibody formation, but data on long-term, repeated exposure are lacking.12
  • Animal and cell findings: repeated daily injections under the skin caused bleeding, inflammation and tissue death at the injection site in rats and dogs, and the peptide produced DNA-damage (genotoxic) signals in mouse pituitary cells and in mice.6
  • Off-target binding: the reactive DAC group may bind cysteines in proteins other than albumin.13
  • People who may be at more risk: FDA says effects such as dizziness, low blood pressure, loss of coordination and a faster heart rate warrant further study, because they could change how people at risk of falls or with heart disease would need to be monitored.4
  • Wrong substance: FDA calls inconsistent naming of CJC-1295 substances a safety risk, because a patient may get a different substance than the one ordered.14 A product seized in Norway in 2009 and matching CJC-1295 held a 29-amino-acid peptide without the DAC.1520
  • Product quality: Health Canada warns that unauthorized injectable peptides may contain too much, too little or none of the active ingredient, unlisted ingredients, or contaminants such as bacteria, fungi and endotoxins, and may cause infections and allergic reactions.16

Limits of the safety data

The human safety record rests on 63 people who received the drug, 87% of them men, and 73% received only one dose.4 All were healthy adults in small, short studies.11 Side effects in people with illnesses, in children and over months or years of use have not been studied.4 A 2026 narrative review of injectable peptides in sports medicine describes the safety profiles of growth hormone secretagogues such as CJC-1295 as uncertain.17 General background on reading this kind of evidence is in Peptide safety basics.

Frequently asked questions

Did someone die in a CJC-1295 trial?

Yes. In ConjuChem's phase 2 trial in people with HIV lipodystrophy, a participant reported chest discomfort 2 hours after an 11th weekly dose; an ECG confirmed an acute myocardial infarction (heart attack), and the participant died about an hour later.3 The attending physician's most likely explanation was asymptomatic coronary artery disease with plaque rupture and occlusion.34 The trial was terminated, ConjuChem withdrew CJC-1295 from clinical trials in 2006, and the data have not been published.35

Is CJC-1295 safe long term? Can it cause cancer?

Long-term safety in people is unknown, because the published studies were small and short and most participants received one dose.4 FDA found no carcinogenicity studies, notes DNA-damage signals in mouse pituitary cells and in mice, and says pituitary overgrowth and tumors with long-term use cannot be ruled out.6 Approved growth hormone labels warn of an increased risk of tumors, and FDA has no data showing CJC-1295 would avoid that risk.7

Does CJC-1295 cause a fast heart rate?

It was reported. In 12 healthy men given one injection, heart rate rose, and the rise was larger at higher doses.719 FDA lists increased heart rate, along with systemic vasodilatory reactions such as flushing and brief low blood pressure, among serious adverse events associated with CJC-1295.81 FDA also says effects such as dizziness, low blood pressure and a faster heart rate warrant further study, because they could change how people with heart disease or at risk of falls would need to be monitored.4

Has CJC-1295 been studied in women?

Barely. Of the 63 people given it in the published studies, 55 (87%) were men, and two of the three studies enrolled only men.4910 Effects and side effects in women are therefore largely unstudied, and FDA notes the safety profile may differ in other populations.4 There is also no safety information on use in children.11
