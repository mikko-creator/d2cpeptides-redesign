What is BPC-157?

BPC-157 is a synthetic peptide of 15 amino acids, described as a partial sequence of a protein isolated from human gastric juice.13 First reported in 1993, it has been studied mostly in rats.23 Human studies are few, small and short, and no BPC-157 medicine is approved in any country.32

What sources report

What BPC-157 is

BPC-157 is a chain of 15 amino acids: Gly-Glu-Pro-Pro-Pro-Gly-Lys-Pro-Ala-Asp-Asp-Ala-Gly-Leu-Val, or GEPPPGKPADDAGLV in one-letter code.4 PubChem lists the free base as C62H98N16O22, molar mass 1419.5 g/mol, compound ID 9941957.5 Its CAS registry number is 137525-51-0.6 Researchers describe it as a partial sequence of body protection compound, a protein isolated from human gastric juice.1 The literature first reported it in 1993.2

FDA treats BPC-157 free base and its acetate salt as distinct active ingredients, though both contain the same peptide.2 It has also seen several salts and derivatives sold under the one name.2 Researchers will find BPC-157 for sale here as a 5 mg vial of freeze-dried powder.

How it is thought to work

In endothelial (vessel-lining) cells and in rats with reduced blood flow to a hind leg, BPC-157 raised VEGFR2, a receptor for vessel growth (2017).13 It also switched on the VEGFR2-Akt-eNOS signaling pathway, and treated rats regained blood flow faster.13 In rat tendon cells, it increased outgrowth, survival under stress and migration, likely through the FAK-paxillin pathway (2011).1 FDA cautions that the molecule's targets have not been identified, which makes the reported effects hard to judge.2

BPC-157 resisted breakdown by gastric juice in the lab, but it clears the blood fast.2 In rats and dogs, unchanged BPC-157 had a half-life under 30 minutes.19 It was broken into small fragments and then single amino acids (2022).19

Animal research

Most of the evidence comes from animal studies, chiefly in rats.326 After the Achilles tendon was cut, rats given BPC-157 had stronger tendons, better walking scores and better tissue repair than rats given saline (2003).18 A tendon cut from the heel bone did not heal back on its own in rats but did with BPC-157 (2006).23 Other rat studies reported better healing of a fully cut quadriceps over 72 days and of a cut knee ligament over 90 days.2425

FDA's review adds gut findings in rats: colon fistulas about 40% smaller from day 3, and NSAID-induced stomach and intestinal lesions more than 75% smaller.2 A 2025 systematic review of orthopaedic research counted 35 preclinical studies among the 36 it included.26

Human studies

Human data are few and weak: a 2026 systematic review found three studies with 30 people in total, all before-and-after designs with no control group.3 It rated the certainty of evidence very low and said BPC-157 should not be used clinically outside regulated, high-quality trials.3

Study Who What was reported
Knee pain, 2021 (chart review) 16 of 17 patients reached at follow-up 11 of 12 given BPC-157 alone reported significant improvement; no standard measurement tools27
Interstitial cystitis, 2024 (pilot) 12 women 10 of 12 reported complete symptom resolution after one procedure; no adverse events28
IV safety, 2025 (pilot) 2 healthy adults No change in heart, liver, kidney, thyroid or blood sugar markers20
Ulcerative colitis, 2005 (meeting abstract) 53 people, randomized Scores fell 3.2 points with BPC-157 and 1.6 with placebo; no clear difference between groups2

FDA concluded there is a lack of evidence that BPC-157 works for ulcerative colitis, a disease with several FDA-approved treatments.2 A University of Arkansas pilot after rotator cuff surgery (target 30 people) was not yet recruiting in October 2026.29 Its record states that BPC-157 has not yet been studied in formal human clinical trials.29

BPC-157 is also sold blended with TB-500 as the Wolverine stack. For other compounds studied in tissue repair, see the healing research overview.

Side effects

Side effects in people are not well described: FDA notes the human studies were short and small and gave limited safety information.2

Reported in people

  • FDA's adverse event database (FAERS, searched through December 2025) held three reports, all involving injected BPC-157.2
  • One report was an injection-site reaction, and one was shortness of breath.2
  • The third described skin and gum darkening after a research-labeled product that also contained TB-500.2 The reaction returned when the product was used again.2
  • FDA could not determine whether BPC-157 caused any of the three.2
  • Healthy volunteers given BPC-157 enemas most often reported headache and flatulence, with no obvious difference from placebo in frequency or severity.2

Seen in animals

  • Mild irritation in a local tolerance test, and lower creatinine levels in dogs given 2 mg/kg daily, which reversed after 2 weeks off the drug (2020).30
  • In FDA's reading of 28-day injection studies: clotting-test changes (aPTT shorter in rats, longer in dogs) and liver-related signals.2

Warnings

  • FDA says injected or nasal BPC-157 may trigger immune reactions, which clumping and impurities could worsen.2
  • FDA identified no cancer (carcinogenicity) studies, and the published reproduction study did not cover a full reproductive cycle.2
  • Health Canada warns that unauthorized injectable peptides may cause infections and allergic reactions, and may contain high-risk ingredients or contaminants, listed on the label or not.31

General precautions are in peptide safety basics.

Reconstitution and storage

No approved BPC-157 product exists, so no official label sets mixing or storage rules.2 FDA describes the free base as a white to off-white freeze-dried powder that dissolves in water at 5 mg/mL, citing supplier data.2 Supplier data cited by FDA report the powder keeps about 3 weeks at room temperature, with long-term storage dry and below -18 °C.2 After mixing, other supplier figures cited by FDA give 2 to 3 weeks at 4 °C and 3 to 4 months at -20 °C.2 FDA adds that pH, heat, concentration and impurities can make such peptides clump or degrade.2

Regulatory status

United States

BPC-157 is not FDA-approved, is not part of any approved drug and has no USP or National Formulary monograph.2 FDA notes it is marketed in US dietary supplement capsules and tablets.2 The Defense Department's Operation Supplement Safety program says it is not a dietary ingredient and puts it on the DoD Prohibited Dietary Supplement Ingredients List.35

Its compounding status has shifted: FDA moved BPC-157 into category 2, substances with significant safety risks, in September 2023.14 By April 2026, FDA listed it among category 2 substances whose nominators had withdrawn them.9 FDA's safety note stayed posted: possible immune reactions, impurity problems and too little data to know whether it would harm people.9

For its July 23, 2026 advisory committee meeting, FDA evaluated BPC-157 for ulcerative colitis and proposed leaving it off the 503A Bulks List.102 Committee members disagreed, voting 8 to 6 with 1 abstention to add both the free base and the acetate.17 The vote is advisory, and FDA says it will decide only after considering it.2 FDA's meeting page showed no final decision as of October 24, 2026.10

A "research use only" label does not change the law.34 In a warning letter dated June 12, 2023, FDA treated a website's BPC-157 injectables and nasal sprays as unapproved new drugs.34 The site, FDA said, showed they were meant for people even though they carried "research purpose only" labels.34

Other countries

FDA found no approved BPC-157 product in any country, and BPC-157 is not in the European, Japanese or International Pharmacopeias.2 Health Canada lists it among unauthorized injectable peptide drugs it has seized.32 It says such products are illegal in Canada and that "For Research Use Only" labelling does not make them legal.32 Australia made BPC-157 a prescription-only (Schedule 4) medicine from June 1, 2024, so possessing it without authority is illegal.33 New Zealand's Medicines Classification Committee recommended prescription-medicine status in May 2023, noting very little human data.8

Sport

WADA's 2026 Prohibited List names BPC-157 in class S0, non-approved substances, prohibited both in and out of competition.7 WADA first listed it on January 1, 2022.8 USADA's advisory on the 2027 list, in force from January 1, 2027, keeps it prohibited under S0.37 In 2017, a US sports drug-testing laboratory identified BPC-157 in confiscated vials and validated a urine test that detects it at 0.1 ng/mL.36

Frequently asked questions

What are the key differences between TB500 and BPC-157?

BPC-157 has 15 amino acids and is described as part of a gastric juice protein.1 TB-500 is LKKTETQ, a seven-amino-acid fragment of thymosin beta-4, and FDA has found no human exposure data for it.9 WADA bans both at all times, BPC-157 under class S0 and TB-500 under S2.7 In July 2026, FDA's compounding advisory committee reviewed BPC-157 for ulcerative colitis and TB-500 for wound healing.10 Read more: What is TB-500?.

What does the peptide BPC-157 do?

In people, no effect has been established: USADA notes that no human clinical trial shows BPC-157 works for any diagnosis or treatment.12 In rats, it has been reported to improve healing of cut tendons, ligaments and muscle.182524 Cell and rat studies link it to blood-vessel growth signals and to tendon-cell migration.131 FDA says its molecular targets are still unidentified.2

What are the differences between BPC-157 and sermorelin?

Sermorelin is a growth hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH) analogue that FDA approved as GEREF, including for idiopathic growth hormone deficiency in children with growth failure.11 Its maker discontinued GEREF in 2008, and FDA later found it was not withdrawn for safety or effectiveness reasons.11 No BPC-157 product is approved in any country.2 WADA lists sermorelin under S2 and BPC-157 under S0.7 Read more: What is Sermorelin?.

Is BPC-157 approved by the FDA for human use?

No. FDA's 2026 review states that neither BPC-157 free base nor its acetate is a component of any FDA-approved drug, and that no BPC-157 product is approved in any country.2 Neither form has a USP or National Formulary monograph.2 FDA also warns that injected or nasal BPC-157 may carry a significant risk of immune reactions.2

Why is compounded BPC-157 hard to get?

In September 2023, FDA placed BPC-157 in category 2, its group of compounding substances with significant safety risks.14 That month it told a pharmacy that drugs compounded with BPC-157 acetate were not eligible for the federal compounding exemptions.15 The nominations were later withdrawn, but a pharmacy still needs a USP monograph, an approved-drug component or a 503A Bulks List entry, and BPC-157 has none.9162 An advisory committee voted in July 2026 to recommend listing it, and FDA had not decided as of October 24, 2026.1710

How long does it take BPC-157 to work?

This is not known for people. The human studies in a 2026 systematic review were three small before-and-after studies without control groups, rated very low certainty, so they cannot show when, or whether, it acts.3 The knee-pain study relied on patient reports without standard measurement tools.27 In the 2003 rat tendon study, differences were measured between days 1 and 14.18 In rats and dogs, unchanged BPC-157 leaves the blood quickly, with a half-life under 30 minutes.19

Can BPC-157 cause liver damage?

This is not known for people, because FDA finds too little clinical safety information to characterize BPC-157.2 In a 2-person IV pilot, liver blood tests did not change.20 In rats given liver-damaging challenges (surgery, restraint stress or a toxic chemical), BPC-157 prevented liver cell death (necrosis) and the rise in liver enzymes.2 Yet FDA's reading of 28-day injection studies in rats and dogs noted liver-related signals, including higher ALT, glucose and triglyceride levels.2

Does BPC-157 help with COVID?

There is no human evidence that it does. A 2021 hypothesis paper proposed BPC-157 for COVID-19, based mainly on animal data, and said clinical trials in people would be needed first.21 As of October 24, 2026, none of the four BPC-157 studies registered on ClinicalTrials.gov concerned COVID-19.22
